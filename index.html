We are a student robotics team in Waukee, Iowa. We take the state's own crash records, updated twice a month, and group them into the stretches of road where drivers keep hitting animals — so you can check your route before you drive it, and so road project designers can see exactly where a fix like fencing or a crossing structure would do the most good.

A corridor is a group of crashes within about 4 km of each other. Corridors with fewer than 3 crashes are not listed. Coordinates are the centre of the group, not an exact crash location. Enter an Iowa ZIP code to see the 10 corridors closest to it.

How we built it

Where the numbers come from

1 / The data

Every animal-related crash reported to police in Iowa since January 2025, pulled directly from the Iowa Department of Transportation's public crash-data service and refreshed twice a month. The service records where, when and how bad — but not which animal. So we count animal crashes, not deer crashes. We did not estimate or invent any of it.

2 / Grouping

We grouped crashes that happened within about 4 km of each other into one corridor, then ranked corridors by how many crashes they contain.

3 / What changed

Our first version shaded the map by where deer live. A wildlife biologist at the Iowa DNR told us crash risk follows traffic volume, not deer density. So we rebuilt it around crash counts instead.

4 / What it can't do

It shows a pattern from the past. It cannot tell you an animal is there right now, it cannot tell you which animal, and it only contains crashes someone reported to police. Research on deer crashes suggests about 40% are never reported, so the real totals are higher than these. Iowa DOT adds crashes as reports come in, so the last few weeks are always incomplete.

What we're doing with this

Making the case for the fix that actually works

What the research says works

Wildlife fencing combined with underpasses or overpasses reduces collisions with large mammals by 80–100% when built together. Fencing alone just displaces the problem to where the fence ends; crossing structures alone, without fencing, don't reduce collisions at all. Structures have to be sized and placed for the species that actually needs them.

What we're not pretending an app can do

A phone alert can raise awareness. It won't reliably make drivers slow down in a high-risk zone, and pushing people to slow down without changing the road can backfire — mixing fast and slow traffic on the same two-lane road raises the risk of head-on collisions from impatient passing.

Read the brief we're taking to Iowa DOT → Corridor data plus the evidence above, packaged as a one-page case for an engineering study — not a consumer warning.
Cost, compared

What fixing this would actually cost

—

Fencing has to be paired with a crossing structure — a fence alone just blocks animals rather than getting them across safely. Below, we compare that against a red, high-friction road surface, at different lengths of coverage and four grit options — the cheaper alternatives make this kind of fix realistic at more corridors, faster.

Calcined bauxite

Bauxite ore baked at 1,000–1,500°C for hardness. Imported. FHWA's reference material for high-friction surfacing — installed at thousands of curve and ramp sites nationwide since the 2010s.

Taconite

Leftover rock from Minnesota's iron mines. Being field-tested by MnDOT right now on real low-volume roads near Duluth as a bauxite substitute — about 6% less grip, no faster wear over time.

Steel slag

A byproduct of steel manufacturing. Tested as a friction-surface aggregate at the National Center for Asphalt Technology's track and in Indiana DOT-funded research.

Flint chat

Leftover chert gravel from old lead-zinc mining in the Missouri–Oklahoma–Kansas tri-state district. Tested as a friction-surface aggregate in Oklahoma DOT-funded research.

Bauxite's $25–35/sq yd is a real, commercial state-DOT price. The other three don't have a published commercial price yet — still pilot-stage — so they share one conservative estimate below, marked *.

Where this comes from

We talked to a road ecologist. It changed the plan.

In September 2026 we spoke with Marcel Huijser, a road ecologist who has spent decades researching wildlife-vehicle collisions and the measures that reduce them — fencing, crossing structures, and the economics of building them — for transportation agencies across the US. He's also the person who told us static warning signs, and even location-specific alerts like the one this site can show, don't reliably reduce collisions on their own.

His publications, including the fencing and crossing-structure research behind the numbers on this page, are online: mphetc.com/publications.
